Why it wins.

Six things that are true today. No footnotes, no roadmap hand-waving.

🚫🔊

No speakers in the air

Nothing to amplify means no amplified-sound permit and nothing to file a volume complaint about. The music is only in earbuds.

📴

Fully offline

Runs on a bare LAN. The Mac itself — or a ~$30 travel router — is the whole network. Zero internet required.

🙅

No app for guests

No download, no signup, no permissions dance. Join the Wi-Fi, scan, done. Works on iPhone and Android browsers.

🎧

Better, consistent audio

Your direct signal in everyone's own earbuds — no muddy room PA, no dead spots. Every guest hears the same clean feed.

🔒

Plays on a locked phone

Keeps going when the screen's off, in another tab, or while they text friends. Guests aren't stuck babysitting the browser.

📈

It actually scales

It's plain HTTP — encode once, every phone fetches the same chunks. The ceiling is your Wi-Fi access point, ~100 on a good router.

Here's the honest mechanism: you can't actively sync a locked iPhone, so we don't pretend to. Instead every phone sits behind the same stable buffer — a stable buffer is the sync. Target: the room drops together. Not phone-to-phone phase-lock, not the “same instant.”

For the skeptic

No app, and it still scales.

“Browser audio to a hundred phones” sounds like it shouldn't hold up. It does — because partyparty serves static HTTP chunks. Encode the stream once; every phone just fetches the same files, exactly like loading a web page. The real ceiling is how many clients your Wi-Fi access point can hold — roughly 100 on a good router.

The clever-sounding alternatives are the traps:

  • ✕Multicast: browsers can't do it — you'd need a native app on every phone. That kills the “no app” promise.
  • ✕WebRTC: real-time, but the connection dies the moment an iPhone locks or backgrounds. Dancefloor over.
